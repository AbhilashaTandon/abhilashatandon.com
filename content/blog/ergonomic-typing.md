## QWERTY: The OG

The design of the QWERTY keyboard layout can be traced to Christopher Latham Sholes, who along with his colleagues, invented the machine known as the "Type-Writer", the mechanical version of the modern keyboard. Many legends are told about the origin of Sholes's seemingly arbitrary key placements. A common claim is that the key placement was designed so that frequent two letter combinations in English such as "th", "he", "in", and "en", would be far apart from each other on the keyboard. Some claim this was done to [[intentionally slow typists down](http://www.straightdope.com/columns/read/221/was-the-qwerty-keyboard-purposely-designed-to-slow-typists) (for what purpose I don't know) while others claim this was done to prevent keyjamming, which was a common problem with early typewriters where one key would get stuck on an adjacent one and prevent it from depressing[1](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-qwerty-keyboard-will-never-die-where-did-the-150-year-old-design-come-from-49863249/). 

However, both of these theories have some major issues, due to the fact that "er" and "de" are very common in English, yet their corresponding keys are right next to each other. [This paper](https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/server/api/core/bitstreams/dc434be9-80cd-499b-a984-f9fa35954c3b/content) tracks the evolution of the QWERTY keyboard and sheds light on the issues with these theories, as well as offering an alternative that explains other oddities of the layout. They claim that an early ancestor to the QWERTY layout took inspiration from an earlier attempt at a keyboard based on a piano, with the black keys containing the start of the alphabet in order, and the white keys containing the rest in reverse order. Sholes then took this layout and moved the vowels and Y to the top row, along with punctuation. This explains why most vowels are found in the top row, and the sequence of almost alphabetic letters in the home row DFGHJKL.
